THE UNWRITTEN ONES
EPISODE ONE
One Night Only
♦
It started in the dark, the way the worst of it always would, with a child
singing.
A small voice, off-key, a song that had lost most of itself somewhere along the
way and kept going anyway. Names are knots. Names are thread. Pull too hard
— you disappear. The kind of tune a kid learns without being taught, that comes
from nowhere and stays for good.
Then light.
Strung bulbs, swaying warm over a striped archway, and above them,
spelled out in glass and filament: GIDEON'S CARNIVAL OF ENDINGS &
WONDERS. A calliope playing something that was almost the right song.
Wagons painted the color of brand-new circus dreams, years from fading. And
over the gate, patient in the bulbs, the promise that the whole thing turned on:
ONE NIGHT ONLY.
Under the arch stood a man in a showman's coat.
Gideon Marr wore his sixty-odd years the way he wore the coat — like both
had fit him twenty pounds ago. Bone-thin now, the fabric hanging off his
shoulders in soft apology. But the eyes were quick and the eyes were warm, and
if you didn't look too long you'd never catch the part of him that was sorry about
something.
In front of him: a girl. Fifteen, maybe. Backpack over one shoulder, a library
book tucked under her arm with the corners gone soft from too much rereading.
She was counting out dollar bills, damp from her hand, and she was doing it
carefully, the way you count when the count matters.
"You like stories, don't you," Gideon said.
She nodded. Hesitant first. Then not.
"This one's about a girl who got to be anything she wanted." Warm as a porch
light, that voice. He tore her ticket himself and pressed it into her palm like a
secret.
She went in — under the arch, into the light and the sugar-smell and the
music that was almost right. She glanced back once, delighted, the way you look
back at the person who gave you the thing. And the bright took her.
Gideon waved. She waved back. And then she was gone into it, and the smile
stayed up on his face on its own, the way a light stays on in a room nobody's in
anymore.
He covered a cough with his hat. Fit it back on his head without breaking
stride, because salesmen don't cough.
Behind him the ticket booth opened, and a worker stepped out — nineteen at
the most, face painted over in silver stars, eyes a hundred years too old for the

face they were parked in. The worker looked at Gideon. Gideon looked back.
Neither of them said a word, but there was a whole argument in the nothing
between them, and it was clear from the set of both their mouths that they'd
each had it before and each already lost.
Gideon tipped his hat. Then he walked off into his carnival, whistling the gate
tune a half-step truer than the calliope could manage.
Somewhere, low and close, something creaked. Like a book, opening its
spine.

Across an ocean and a whole ordinary world away, Leo London sat up in bed
before he was awake.
One hand had already clamped down over his own wrist, hard, like
something had bitten him there. Nothing had. There was never anything there.
There was just the reflex, night after night, his body reaching to hold a wound it
couldn't find.
He breathed. He counted the breaths, because counting was supposed to
help. Counting got him to four. Four was where it always came apart.
The moon came through cheap blinds and laid the room out in pale strips —
the posters peeling at one corner each, the sketchbooks stacked into a low
barricade around a jar of broken pencils. Thirteen years old and already
keeping walls between himself and the dark.
On the nightstand sat a feather on a leather cord. Gray-white, edged in black,
too big for any bird this town had ever grown. Beside it lay a picture, half-drawn:
three figures holding hands under a sky nobody had finished. And a fourth
figure, scratched out — erased so hard the paper had torn.
Leo picked up the feather. He didn't put it on. He just held it, his thumb
finding the groove worn smooth into the spine of it from a hundred nights of
exactly this.
Down the hall the fridge shuddered, caught itself, settled into its hum.
"I'm awake," Leo said, under his breath. He said it like a password. Like
something might be listening for it, and might need to be told.
He lay back down. Pulled the blanket to his chin. His eyes stayed open.
They stayed open a long time.

Morning came to the kitchen the way it came to everything in that house —
reluctantly, and without much to show for it.
Faded floral wallpaper. A fridge armored in magnets and old school drawings
nobody had taken down in two years. A calendar still flipped to the wrong
month, as if turning the page were a thing this family had run out of the strength
to do.

burning eggs that nobody had asked for. Eighteen and lean, hoodie half-zipped,

milk and carried it down the hall, and Leo peeled up out of his chair and
followed her out, and Carter was left standing there holding a spatula and three
breakfasts that nobody in this house was going to eat.
His jaw worked. Whatever was climbing up his throat, he swallowed it whole.
Down the hall the fridge clanked and took up its hum again, strained now,
uneven, like it too was tired of holding this place together.

In the living room Carter knelt at the old side table and took a slow breath
before he opened it, the way a man breathes before lifting something heavy.
The drawer was a museum of arrears. Unopened envelopes. FINAL NOTICE
printed in a cheerful red that had clearly never met the people it was
threatening. And taped to the lamp, in their mother's handwriting, a sign: DO
NOT THROW OUT — guarding, with her whole authority, a lamp worth about
eight dollars.
He pulled the key from the cord around his neck. Lifted a battered metal
lockbox out of the drawer and sat down on the rug with it, cross-legged, a kid
about to do again the exact thing he'd sworn to himself he was done doing.
Inside: eighteen dollars and fifty-seven cents, in wrinkled bills and pennies
gone warm from the box. Two wedding rings, worn smooth. A photo of the
whole family at a fair, all of them, smiling like people who don't know yet.
And a ticket stub, faded soft: GIDEON'S CARNIVAL OF ENDINGS &
WONDERS.
He turned it over. He'd turned it over a hundred times. On the back, in their
mother's looping hand, the ink gone the rust color that old ink goes:
If it comes back before I do —
And then nothing. The sentence just walked off the edge of the paper and
didn't come back.
He ran his thumb over the torn edge of it, careful, like the paper might wake
up if he wasn't. Upstairs, footsteps. He closed the box quick, locked it, slid it
home — practiced, all of it, a routine he'd worn smooth same as Leo and the
feather.

The knock came at the front door like a debt collector in a good mood. Three
raps, cheerful.
Leo got there first and opened the door on nobody at all. Winter light. An
empty street.
He found it tacked to the porch rail, damp along one edge: a poster. Elegant
script curling over a drawing of a striped tent.
GIDEON'S CARNIVAL OF ENDINGS & WONDERS — ONE NIGHT ONLY.
And beneath that, smaller, and somehow more patient: RETURN WHAT WAS
FORGOTTEN. REMEMBER WHAT WAS TAKEN.
He carried it inside flat on both palms, like water he didn't want to spill, and
laid it on the hall table. And the ink moved. Barely. Breathing, almost — a slow
rise and fall across the paper, like it knew it was being looked at and was
deciding how much to let him see.
Under his shirt, against his chest, the feather woke up. A tingle, low and
electric.
Leo went very still. He dug the half-drawn picture out of his hoodie pocket
and unfolded it on the table beside the poster.
The symbol on the tent flap. The symbol in his own unfinished sky. The same
hand had drawn both, and the hand was his.
"Who was it?" Ava, coming down the stairs.
He folded the picture away fast. "Wrong house."
First lie of the day. It wouldn't be the last.

The wagons came in on a whistle — thin, off-key, the kind of sound a town
half-remembers from a dream it had once and never told anybody about.
They rolled up the far end of Main Street, creaking and swaying, the paint
the color of old circus dreams now, wheels tall as doors. Carnival workers
walked alongside in vintage vests, faces painted bright, eyes not painted
anything at all. Down the block, doors opened. Locals drifted out onto their
porches. Kids materialized from nowhere the way kids do, pointing, cheering,
and parents lifted their phones to catch it.
A little girl tugged her mother's sleeve.
"Mommy, it never left. It just forgot to show up last year."
The mother laughed. And then she didn't. She looked down at her daughter,
and then out at the wagons rolling in, and she went hunting through herself for
a single memory of them — and found no memory of them at all.
"...Right," she said, soft.

The carnival at full glow was a beautiful thing, and that was the trap of it.
Strung lights swaying warm over the striped tents. The calliope playing its
almost-right tune. Families streaming in through the gate, sugared and
laughing and alive, and over all of them in the bulbs the same patient promise:
ONE NIGHT ONLY.
Leo rolled up on his bike out of breath, shoes untied, and dropped the
kickstand and looked up. And kept looking up.
A grin arrived on his face before he had any say in it — and then he caught
himself and wiped it off with his sleeve, quick, like somebody might have seen a
thirteen-year-old be happy and hold it against him.

Leo's hand went to his chest on its own. Against his skin the feather was

EPISODE TWO
Every Image Has a Price

Sound came back before anything else did — the calliope, except it was
playing backward now, the notes dripping in reverse like water finding its way
up a drain.
The three of them lay sprawled on the midway. The same midway. And that
was the worst part, that it was the same.
Except.
The strung lights hung unlit in the air, motionless, like fireflies somebody had
caught and frozen. The tents leaned at angles that hurt to look at directly,
angles a tent had no business holding. And overhead there was no sky. Where
the stars should have been there were pages — a ceiling of them, vast and slow-
turning, wheeling without any wind to move them. Off to one side the Ferris
wheel turned too, riderless, in perfect silence, going around and around for
nobody at all.
Carter came up first, scrambling, dragging Leo behind him by a fistful of
hoodie.
"Ava? Ava —"
"Here." She was already sitting up. "I'm here."
"You okay?" He had both of them by now, one hand on each. "Look at me.
Both of you. Are you okay?"
They ended up standing close without deciding to, three of them in a ring,
backs together, facing out. It was old instinct and nobody had ever taught it to
them.
"This is it," Leo said, small. "This is the place from my —"
"Don't." Carter didn't let him finish. "Don't say dream. Just — don't."
Down the midway, a games booth stirred.
Something shuffled out from behind the counter of it. A man, more or less —
a man made out of smudged pencil-lines, his face half rubbed away, like a
drawing somebody had started to erase and then lost the heart for. A scrap of
paper floated at his eye level, patient as a waiter. As they watched, a single line
wrote itself across the scrap: Sign here, and rest.
The man reached out. Touched it. His mouth moved around something — a
name, worn down to almost nothing, just the ghost of the shape of one.
And then he smoothed away. There was no other word for it. Like a thumb
dragged through wet ink. Where he had been standing there was nothing, and
the scrap that had held his name floated up and up and filed itself neatly into the
turning pages overhead.
Nobody wanted to say the word afraid, so nobody said anything at all.

In the dark between the booths there were more of them, standing, waiting for

"They're people," Leo said, quiet.
"They were readers. Then they were characters. Now they're waiting."
"Waiting for what?" Carter asked.
"Whatever the Book does with pages it's finished."

"Rule of the road, small one." The creature tugged his sleeve, and for once it

was no comedy left anywhere in it.
"Off the path. Now."

Carter's eyes opened first. He scrambled up on old instinct and counted —

"We're out," Ava said, hoarse.
Beyond the treeline, where a carnival had stood, was a trampled ring of

Three weeks later.
A different kitchen — smaller, rented, alive. Sun through clean glass.
Drawings on the fridge again, new ones. And at the stove, flipping something
with unearned confidence, stood Leo. Smoke. A little. Nobody panicked.
"That one's Carter's."
"You're burning them," Carter said from the table, not getting up, a miracle
in itself.
"I'm caramelizing them."
"He learned that word yesterday," Ava said, passing through, snagging juice.
"He's been waiting."
Pancakes. Bad ones. The griddle-smell of a promise kept late, by the wrong
cook, which is the only way some promises survive. Carter accepted a scorched
pancake with the gravity of a man accepting a medal. Ate it. Didn't fix it. Didn't
fix anything.
By the window, Ava settled with a new sketchbook, its first page already
worked: Brim. Whole. Wings full, coal lit, mid-bow, courtly and ridiculous.
Underneath, in her steadiest hand, four letters. And no dates.

Her pencil stopped. One breath.

means everything and admits nothing. She went back to her shading and kept
the words. The feeling under them held her weight fine.

A small room, honestly slept in. Homework half-done on the desk. The half-

scratched-out fourth, left exactly as it was. Some pages you keep without

Leo, in bed, reached for the lamp. And paused.
Across the room, on the closet shelf, wrapped in an old pillowcase and tied
with Miren-grade knots, sat a book-shaped bundle. Quiet. Kept. Three weeks,
and it hadn't so much as rustled. It had a keeper now.
Leo looked at it a long moment, the way you look at a locked door you own
the key to. Then he turned off the light.
Darkness. The ordinary kind. A fridge humming somewhere, at peace. A car
passing. A house holding a family that fit it.
And on the closet shelf, from one fold of the pillowcase, a single ink-mote
rose, drifted, caught a thread of streetlight — and for half a second, no longer
than a heartbeat, it glowed. Faint. Gold. A coal that refuses to quit. Then it
settled, unhurried, the way a small courtly thing settles in for the night.
Silence.
Not gone. Held.
